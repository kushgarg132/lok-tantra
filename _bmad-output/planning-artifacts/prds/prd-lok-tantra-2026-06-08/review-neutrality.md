# Adversarial Review — Political Neutrality & Sensitivity

## Overall verdict
The §5 guardrail is well-intentioned and correctly identifies the right *axis* of risk (structural/chronological treatment, no editorial curation), but it is written almost entirely as a **layout/visual-prominence rule** — it says nothing about *content selection, framing, presentation order, or shareability*, which is where most real-world "this platform is biased" complaints actually originate. As written, a critic, party IT cell, or journalist could comply with every sentence of §5 to the letter while still producing screenshots, profile pages, and "career path" narratives that read as politically charged. The guardrail needs to grow from "don't visually favor anyone" into "don't let *any* dimension of the experience — content, framing, sequencing, or shareable artifact — read as commentary," with testable consequences attached to specific FRs, not just a constraints paragraph that sits apart from them.

## Findings

- **critical** Accountability Data has no stated *comparative symmetry* principle, only a sourcing one (§5 Source-Verifiability; FR-5) — A profile showing "12 pending criminal cases, ₹47 Cr declared assets" for one named, currently-serving politician is technically "just facts from ADR/MyNeta," but the *act of foregrounding it on a graph node that is centered on the PM* (FR-1) inevitably reads as "look how compromised this specific person is" — especially when screenshotted out of context (SM-2 explicitly optimizes for sharing). §5 only requires source attribution and bans "inference, ranking, scoring, or commentary" — it never requires that Accountability Data be presented with *equal depth, equal visual rank, and equal framing language* across every Person Node regardless of party, current relevance, or how "newsworthy" their case count is. Without that, two politicians with similar legal records from different parties could end up presented asymmetrically simply because one is more "in the news" and thus more likely to be the entry point users tap (UJ-1 is literally "taps a minister's name he just saw in the news"). *Fix:* Add to §5: "Accountability Data is rendered using an identical template, label set, and framing copy for every Person Node at a given rank — the same fields appear in the same order with the same neutral phrasing whether the Person has zero cases/assets or many, and whether they are currently prominent in the news cycle or not. The system never varies depth, ordering, or visual weight of Accountability Data based on a Person's current media salience, party, or the user's entry point into the graph." Add a corresponding FR-5 consequence: "Accountability Data sections use a fixed, party-agnostic template (same field order, same neutral header copy, e.g. 'Declared assets (source: ADR, as of [date])') applied identically to every profile, including those of figures who are not currently controversial."

- **high** "Career path" (FR-3) has no narrative-neutrality principle attached, despite the user-question itself ("how *did* this person become PM?", §1, UJ-1) being inherently a question about legitimacy/merit/favoritism — FR-3 only specifies *what* renders (chronological Office+date-range steps) and a UX behavior (linking to Time Travel). It says nothing about *how the transitions between steps are framed*. A "traced line showing her rise from state legislator to Union Minister" (UJ-1, climax) is visually and narratively adjacent to "rags to riches" or "favoritism/dynasty" storytelling depending on which transitions are emphasized, what gaps look like (e.g., a long unexplained gap before a sudden jump to a powerful office), and whether the same template is applied to a politician whose path was unconventional (defections, suspensions, court-stayed terms) versus a conventional one. The PRD's own Non-Goals (§7: "will not editorialize on... merit") implicitly concedes this is a risk worth naming for Accountability Data, but never extends the same explicit prohibition to the *career-path narrative form itself* — which is arguably the more seductive vector for implied commentary because it's presented as neutral "structure," not opinion. *Fix:* Add an FR-3 consequence: "Career path steps are rendered as neutral structural facts only (Office, dates, and — where applicable — the constitutional/statutory mechanism of transition, e.g. 'appointed,' 'elected,' 'resigned,' 'term ended') with no narrative framing, value-laden verbs (e.g. 'rose,' 'fell,' 'climbed'), or visual emphasis (e.g., size, glow, animation speed) that implies speed, merit, surprise, or grievance about how a transition occurred. The same template and tone apply whether a transition was conventional or unusual." Also flag in §5 Neutrality: "Career-path framing is a content-neutrality concern, not just a visual-prominence one — copy and iconography must avoid implying judgment about *how* someone attained an Office."

- **high** "Party colors are factual attributes, not endorsements" (§5, locked per decision log) is asserted but not defended at the UI-mechanics level, and is very likely indefensible as a blanket claim — Color is not a neutral encoding channel in a graph visualization the moment two or more parties co-occur on screen (e.g., a coalition cabinet, an opposition bench, or a Time-Travel scrub through an era of frequent floor-crossing). Grouping-by-color is one of the strongest pre-attentive visual cues humans have; a graph that color-codes by party will *visually cluster* allies and *visually separate* rivals regardless of whether that was the design intent — which is exactly the kind of "structure that reads as a statement" a critic would screenshot and caption ("look how LokTantra visually lumps party X together / isolates party Y"). The PRD treats this as settled by precedent ("consistent with how `PartyTracker` ... already treat party colors," §5) but `PartyTracker` is a feature *about* parties, where color-by-party is the entire point — Power Explorer is a feature about *constitutional structure*, where color suddenly becomes the most party-salient visual element on an otherwise structurally-organized canvas. Reusing that precedent without re-examining it in the new context is a category error the guardrail doesn't catch. *Fix:* Either (a) demote party color to a *secondary, optional* encoding (e.g., a small swatch/chip on the node, not a fill/border that drives visual grouping), so the graph's primary visual organization remains strictly hierarchical/chronological as §5 already mandates for layout — and add to §5: "Party affiliation is encoded as a small, consistent badge on every Person Node — never as a primary fill, border, clustering, or layout-driving channel — so that color cannot create visual groupings or oppositions that the graph's structural organization does not itself impose"; or (b) if color-by-party is kept as a primary channel, require an explicit, auditable, alphabetically-or-rank-ordered color assignment scheme published alongside the feature (so no party can be perceived as having been assigned a "good" or "bad" hue), and add that requirement as a testable FR-1/FR-2 consequence. Either way, §5's current one-line assertion needs to become a falsifiable rule, not a description of intent.

- **high** SM-2 ("share rate") actively rewards the production of shareable, decontextualized fragments — exactly the artifact type most likely to be politically misread — yet no FR or guardrail constrains *what a shared fragment looks like* — UJ-1's resolution is explicitly "screenshots the graph to explain it to a friend," and SM-2 turns that into a primary success metric ("how often a graph view or profile is shared/screenshotted/exported"). A screenshot of a single Person Node — say, an opposition leader's profile foregrounding a large, bold "14 pending criminal cases" stat, cropped without the source/date footer or the equally-prominent peer comparison — is a ready-made, out-of-context attack image, and the PRD literally optimizes for producing more of these. SM-C2 only guards against *algorithmic* skew toward controversial figures (i.e., the platform choosing what to surface); it says nothing about the *shape of the artifact* a user organically chooses to crop and share, which is the actual mechanism by which "neutral structural facts" become "politically charged screenshot." *Fix:* Add a guardrail to §5 (or a new FR under 4.2/4.5, e.g. "Shareable exports"): "Any explicit share/export affordance (if built) renders source attribution, last-updated date, and a 'view full profile / full graph context' indicator as part of the exported image itself — so a shared fragment cannot be stripped of the context that makes it neutral. Organic screenshots cannot be controlled, but the in-product layout for Accountability Data and career-path sections is designed so that source/date/context appear *within the natural crop boundary* of a single screen, not below the fold." Also add a corresponding counter-metric note to SM-2: "Share-rate growth driven disproportionately by Accountability Data sections on a small number of figures should trigger a qualitative review — it may indicate the feature is being used as an attack-research tool rather than a civic-understanding tool, which would conflict with §7 Non-Goals."

- **medium** MVP scope (§8.1: national/central layer only, "centered on the PM") creates a structurally-induced asymmetry that §5's "equivalent rank, equivalent treatment" promise cannot actually keep, by the PRD's own design — §5 says "every Office and every Person at an equivalent constitutional rank receives equivalent data depth and visual treatment — no figure or party is foregrounded." But FR-1 *literally centers the default view on the Prime Minister's Office* — a position that, across India's post-1947 history, has been held overwhelmingly by leaders of one or two parties for the large majority of the 79 years the Time Travel range covers (§4.4: "1947 to present"). "Centered on the PM" is presented as a neutral structural choice (it is the apex of the executive), but its *practical effect* — given the historical distribution of who has actually occupied that apex — is that the platform's default, first-impression view will, across most of its historical range, foreground individuals from a small number of parties simply because that is who the Constitution's structure happened to put there. This is not a flaw in the *guardrail's intent*, but it is a gap the guardrail doesn't acknowledge or resolve: "structural" centering and "politically lopsided historical defaults" are not mutually exclusive, and a critic could note that the platform's single most-viewed default screen (SM-1 measures traffic to exactly this surface) will, for most Time-Travel positions, look like it's "about" whichever party has governed longest. *Fix:* Add an explicit acknowledgment + mitigation to §5 or §6: "Centering the default view on the Prime Minister's Office is a structural choice (constitutional apex of the executive), not a partisan one — but because India's historical PM tenure has been concentrated among a small number of parties, the product must actively counterbalance this by ensuring (a) the *default* view emphasizes the Office and its constitutional powers ahead of the current/historical occupant's identity — i.e., the chrome foregrounds 'Prime Minister — Article 75' before it foregrounds '[Name], [Party]' — and (b) Time Travel's UI gives equal navigational weight to all eras, with no default landing date other than 'today,' so historical exploration is user-driven, not platform-curated toward any era." Consider adding this as a testable FR-1 consequence.

- **medium** "Last known state" gap-resolution rule (§3 Time Travel; FR-7) can silently misattribute officeholders across contested transitions, in ways that read as taking a side on disputed political history — The rule "resolve to the most recent Tenure-start at or before the selected date" is clean for ordinary successions, but Indian political history (the very range FR-7 promises, 1947–present) includes periods with contested, overlapping, or rapidly-reversed claims to office (e.g., short-lived governments, President's Rule impositions and revocations, defection-driven floor-crossings, judicially-stayed appointments, and the Emergency-era reshuffles Priya's own journey, UJ-2, specifically scrubs to in 1977). A purely mechanical "last known Tenure-start" resolution could present a contested or briefly-reversed Tenure as settled fact for the in-between dates, and a partisan reading of *which* version of a contested transition the data team encoded as "the" Tenure-start could itself become a controversy — especially for dates that fall inside a dispute window. The PRD treats this purely as a UX/data-modeling question (already "resolved" per the decision log) but never flags it as a *neutrality* concern requiring source-documentation discipline for exactly the contested intervals where it matters most. *Fix:* Add to §5 or as an FR-7 consequence: "For any interval where the 'active Tenure' is genuinely contested in the historical record (e.g., disputed government formations, President's Rule periods, judicially stayed or reversed appointments), the Tenure data itself — not just the UI — must carry a citation to the authoritative source for *why* that Tenure was encoded as active for that range, and the explainer should note when a period was contested rather than presenting 'last known state' as uncontroversial settled fact." This keeps FR-7's elegant resolution rule but prevents it from quietly encoding an editorial judgment about contested history as if it were structural fact.

- **medium** §7 Non-Goals bans "commentary or analysis on unfolding political developments," but FR-2's "drill through a Node's connections" + FR-8's "search by... party" combination effectively builds a tool for constructing real-time political narratives about *current* events, which is in tension with that Non-Goal — A user who searches by party today, drills from a newly-reshuffled minister up to the PM, and reads the "reports to" edges and Accountability Data, is functionally doing live political analysis *through* the tool — UJ-1 is framed as exactly this ("Cabinet reshuffle everyone's talking about," "screenshots the graph to explain it to a friend"). The PRD's Non-Goal language ("not a news or current-events feed... shows structure and history, not commentary on unfolding developments") sounds like it forecloses this, but the actual FRs *are* the mechanism by which users will do precisely that — there's a gap between the stated Non-Goal and what the shipped feature set actually enables and even celebrates (UJ-1's climax is explicitly about a trending current event). This isn't necessarily a problem to "fix" by removing functionality — it's a problem of the Non-Goal overpromising a separation the FRs don't actually maintain, which a critic could point to as inconsistency ("you say you're not about current events, but your flagship user journey is literally about a trending news story"). *Fix:* Soften/clarify §7's Non-Goal to be accurate about what the product actually does: "This is not a news or current-events *feed* — it does not surface, rank, or push unfolding developments to users, and it adds no original commentary on them. It is, however, expected that users will arrive *prompted* by current events (as in UJ-1) and use the tool to understand the *structural* facts behind those events — the guardrail is that LokTantra never frames, ranks, or annotates *which* current events matter or *what they mean*, leaving that interpretation entirely to the user." This makes the Non-Goal defensible and consistent with the FRs rather than appearing to contradict them.

- **low** FR-4's "convention, not codified" label (for Offices/powers without a clean constitutional citation) has no neutrality framing, but conventions are frequently the most politically contested part of how power actually works in India — Several real points of executive power in India operate by convention rather than codified text (e.g., aspects of Cabinet formation, coalition dynamics, certain gubernatorial discretion questions) — and *which* conventions the platform chooses to describe, and how it phrases them, is inherently more interpretively loaded than quoting an Article verbatim. FR-4 only specifies the testable *presence* of a citation-or-label; it says nothing about how "convention, not codified" content is sourced, reviewed, or kept descriptively (rather than normatively) framed — a gap that's easy to miss because it looks like a small UI-state requirement rather than a content-neutrality one. *Fix:* Add an FR-4 consequence: "Where an Office's power is described as 'convention, not codified,' the description is sourced to a neutral, citable reference (e.g., constitutional law scholarship, Supreme Court commentary, parliamentary record) rather than house-written interpretation, and is phrased descriptively ('by convention, X typically occurs') rather than normatively ('X should/must occur') — the same source-verifiability bar as Constitutional Citations applies to convention-based descriptions, which is precisely where unsourced editorial framing is most likely to creep in unnoticed."
